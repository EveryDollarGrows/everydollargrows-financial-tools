EVERYDOLLARGROWS · MONEY LEARNING LAB

Three money decisions to test

Short activities for teens, adult learners and families. Estimate an answer first, use a calculator to test it, then explain the result. No account or personal financial details are needed. Use the invented examples below rather than a learner’s real income or debts.

1. Why dividing groceries by four fails

Learning goal: convert monthly and weekly amounts without losing track of the year. Allow about 10 minutes; learners need multiplication and division.

A fictional household sets a grocery budget of $780 per month for three people. A friend suggests spending $195 each week because $780 ÷ 4 = $195.

  1. What would $195 each week cost over 52 weeks?
  2. How much is the original monthly budget over 12 months?
  3. Find a weekly average that fits the annual budget with the grocery budget calculator.
  4. What is the monthly amount per person? Why is this a comparison rather than a recommendation for what each person should eat?
Worked answers and discussion

$195 × 52 = $10,140. The annual budget is $780 × 12 = $9,360. The four-week shortcut would spend $780 more over a 52-week planning year. The sustainable weekly average is $9,360 ÷ 52 = $180. Monthly per person is $260.

Months do not contain exactly four weeks. Food needs differ by age, health, prices and meals eaten away from home; an even split does not measure those needs. In a five-shopping-day month, use saved carryover or a monthly shopping plan rather than increasing the annual allowance.

2. A goal, a deadline and a tradeoff

Learning goal: compare saving sooner with saving more each week. Allow about 10 minutes; learners need subtraction and division.

Jordan wants $600 available for a holiday and already has $150 set aside. There are 9 weekly deposits left.

  1. Use the weekly holiday savings calculator to find the deposit needed.
  2. Jordan can afford only $35 a week. What total will be available by the deadline, including the $150 already saved?
  3. How much must the target fall if the deadline and $35 deposit stay fixed?
  4. Try 12 weeks instead. What weekly deposit reaches the original target?
Worked answers and discussion

$600 − $150 = $450 still needed. $450 ÷ 9 = $50 per week. At $35 for 9 weeks, Jordan adds $315 and has $465 available. The target must fall by $135. With 12 deposits, $450 ÷ 12 = $37.50 per week.

Changing a deadline, a target or a contribution changes the plan. A calculator shows the tradeoff; it does not create money in the budget. For a longer monthly goal, compare the savings goal calculator at 0% interest, then use the free EDG Savings Goal Planner to record a plan.

3. When a debt payment makes no progress

Learning goal: distinguish interest from principal. Allow about 15 minutes; learners need percentages.

Sam has a fictional $1,200 balance at a nominal annual rate of 12%. The model applies interest monthly before the payment, without fees or new purchases.

  1. Calculate the first month’s interest before opening a tool.
  2. What happens with a $12 monthly payment? Test it in the debt payoff calculator.
  3. Try a $100 payment. How much principal is removed in the first month? How many payments does the model estimate?
  4. At 0% interest and $100 per month, how many payments would be needed?
Worked answers and discussion

The monthly rate is 12% ÷ 12 = 1%. The first month’s interest is $1,200 × 0.01 = $12. Paying $12 leaves the balance unchanged, so the calculator flags that payment as too low. A $100 payment covers $12 interest and reduces principal by $88 in month one. The constant-rate model takes 13 payments, with a reduced final payment. At 0%, the balance takes exactly 12 payments.

Actual credit cards can calculate interest daily and charge fees. Do not treat a model as an actual lender payoff quote. Compare the compound interest calculator to see how the same idea of growth over time works for a positive balance.

Facilitator notes

Ask learners to identify what changes and what stays fixed, then explain the answer in their own words. You can discuss all amounts in one consistent currency; no conversion occurs. Show the assumptions with every result. The activities are practice scenarios, not instructions to borrow or invest.
